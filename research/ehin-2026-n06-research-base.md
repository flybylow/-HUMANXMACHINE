## How to read this file

Every claim carries one of three tags:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[E] Established** | Repeated by several groups or textbook knowledge. |
| **[L] Lab-scale** | Shown in controlled experiments, often by one group, not yet shown in the wild or at scale. |
| **[H] Hypothesis** | A proposal, an interpretation, or this project's own idea. Not yet tested. |

Sources carry one of two marks:

- ✔ means the bibliographic details (authors, journal, year, DOI) were confirmed by search during this session. The full text was not always read.
- ⚠ means the source came from your Notion notes or from memory and was **not** confirmed in this session. Check it before you cite it.

**Two framing rules for the whole file:**

1. "Intelligence" is a framing choice, not a measurement. In this file it means *a graded capacity to sense, keep, process and respond to information* (after Lyon 2006 and Levin 2019). Under that definition clay, microbes, fungi and AI all sit somewhere on one scale. That does not make them equal, and it does not mean any of them thinks.
2. Microcontrollers measure **proxies**: voltage, humidity, temperature, light, CO₂, colour. They do not read thoughts or meanings. Turning a proxy into sound or light is an artistic decision, so the proposal should present it as a method, not as a finding.

## The idea in one message (simplified, 4 November 2026)

> **One score, sung by different voices.**
> Clay from a few places in Asturias, each with its own life, is made into identical vessels that follow the same rules. What you hear is the place.

**Three steps, easy to explain:**
1. **Dig.** Visit 3 to 5 clay sites with a local potter or practitioner. Take clay and a small soil sample from each site, and record the site once with the Mobile Pattern Reader. *No electronics stay in the field.*
2. **Listen.** At LABoral, make identical vessels from each clay and let each site's own life grow in them. Each vessel has one node with three sensors (moisture, light or colour, surface voltage), and the vessels sound together in one room.
3. **Fire.** At the end, fire some vessels so the living record becomes a permanent mineral one. The fired vessels and the recorded sound travel to the shows.

**Deliberately left out (to keep it simple):** year-long field nodes, fungi as a separate strand, an AI layer, and many sensors per vessel. Any of these can return later as an extension.

**Practical answers so far** [H, to confirm with LABoral]:
- *Field electronics for a year:* avoid them. Power, weather, theft and permits make this the riskiest part. The field is where the work begins, not where the nodes live.
- *Transport:* clay and soil travel in sealed, labelled bags. The sites are mostly short drives from Gijón (approximate, not checked: Avilés about 25 km, Oviedo or Faro about 30 km, Colunga about 40 km, Cangas del Narcea about 120 km). Living vessels move as little as possible.
- *Cross-contamination in one room:* some mixing is likely through air, water, hands and tools. To limit it, give each vessel a lid with a breathable filter, use separate tools and water for each site, and keep one sealed control. Perfect separation is not possible outside a clean lab. In the Dirty Lab framing, document any mixing rather than hide it. One vessel could even be shared on purpose ("what happens when places meet").

## Executive summary (one page)

**The idea.** A porous clay vessel is a habitat and a record at the same time. Clays are dug at several sites in Asturias, and each clay is colonised by its own site's living community (bacteria, cyanobacteria, algae, fungi). Every clay is local in its minerals, history and microbes, and universal in its origin (elements made in stars, the same mineral physics everywhere). Each vessel, or each small group of vessels, has its own microcontroller. The microcontroller logs slow proxies of the colony's activity (moisture, light absorbed, CO₂, surface voltage, colour change) and turns them into light and sound. The vessels talk to each other over a local mesh, so there is no central brain. The colony also leaves marks in the clay over months. At the end, some vessels can be fired, so that part of the record becomes permanent, the way a sealed clay token envelope became a record.

**Why the history supports it.**
- *Clay as an active material.* Clay minerals concentrate and protect molecules and microbes, and they catalyse chemistry. Ferris and Orgel grew RNA-like chains up to 55 units long on montmorillonite (1996) [L]. Szostak's group showed that clay speeds up the formation of membrane vesicles about 100-fold (2003) [L]. Stotzky showed in the 1960s that clays buffer pH and support bacteria [E]. That clay *started* life remains a contested hypothesis [H].
- *Life as distributed information processing.* Slime moulds solve mazes (2000) [L]. Bacterial biofilms send electrical waves (2015) [L]. Fungal mycelium shows electrical spiking and rhythms that last for weeks (2024) [L]. The interpretation as "cognition" (Lyon, Levin) is influential and contested [H]. The claims of a "fungal language" have been criticised directly (Blatt et al. 2024).
- *Living ceramics now exist at lab scale.* In 2024 ETH Zurich hosted cyanobacteria and engineered bacteria in porous ceramics for carbon capture and gas sensing [L]. In 2013 the Bristol group showed that fired terracotta and earthenware work as membranes in microbial fuel cells, with the voltage read electronically [L].
- *Fired clay keeps records.* Fired bricks lock in the Earth's magnetic field at the moment of firing (PNAS 2023) [E]. Unglazed pots keep food fats for 7,000 years (Nature 2012) [E]. Clay tokens and envelopes are an early accounting system, although the claim that they were the origin of writing is debated [E/H].

**What is new.** The pieces exist separately: living ceramics in a lab, fired clay as a record in archaeology, bio-art in galleries, sensor meshes in maker culture. As far as this search found, **nobody has grown a living culture on ordinary fired or raw clay vessels, logged it for months with a distributed sensor network, and then fired part of it as a permanent record** [H, novelty not exhaustively verified]. Ward's practice already holds most of the technical parts: ESP32 mesh nodes (MycoMesh), ceramic speaker nodes, a craquelure reader (Singing Bowls), light-into-clay chemistry with firing (Stardirt), a surface pattern reader, and a paste extruder.

**The honest gap.** Ward has no wet-lab training yet. LABoral has no in-house wet lab, so the project needs a biology partner from LABoral's university network and a clear biosafety approach for wild communities (for example, sealed vessels and no deliberate culturing of unknown isolates). The Phase 2 co-production lab can provide the wet-lab side (section 13).

**Host and plan.** **LABoral** (Gijón) is the chosen host. Its expert, Dr Gómez Blas, studies how intelligence "can emerge decentralized through the cooperation of multiple simple elements, rather than relying on a single central processor". That is almost exactly the vessel mesh. LABoral has a fabLAB and a soundlab, and coast, mountain and forest sites nearby. Asturias also has living clay traditions (Faro, Miranda, Llamas del Mouro). For Phase 2, **Kersnikova** (BioTehna wet lab) looks like the best complement. See sections 11 to 13.

**Before drafting:** settle the field practitioner and the clay sites, read the four core papers in full (Dutto 2024, Winfield 2013, Ferris 1996, Blatt 2024), write down the definition of intelligence and the proxy-to-sound method, and draft the budget (fee at most €8,750). Open questions are listed at the end.

# Part A: History

## 1. History in science

A short timeline of how clay, microbes, fungi, cognition and computing came to be studied together. They were mostly separate fields until about 2010.

### 1a. Clay and the origin of life (1940s to now)

| Year | Who | What | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1949–1951 | **J. D. Bernal** (UK) | Proposed that clay mineral surfaces could concentrate and protect the first biological molecules. | 1966, 1982 | **A. Graham Cairns-Smith** (Glasgow) | Went further: clay crystals themselves were the first "genes", copying their defect patterns before organic genes took over. *Genetic Takeover and the Mineral Origins of Life*, Cambridge UP, 1982 ✔ | [H], never mainstream, **contested** |
| 1996 | **James Ferris, Leslie Orgel** et al. | Montmorillonite clay builds RNA-like chains up to 55 units long, compared with about 10 in solution. *Nature* 381:59–61. [doi:10.1038/381059a0](https://doi.org/10.1038/381059a0) ✔ | [L] |
| 2002 | Ferris | Review of 30–50-unit clay-made oligonucleotides. *Orig. Life Evol. Biosph.* 32:311. doi:10.1023/A:1020543312109 ⚠ | [L] |
| 2003 | **Hanczyc, Fujikawa, Szostak** (Harvard) | Montmorillonite speeds vesicle (proto-cell) formation about 100-fold, and clay with RNA attached ends up inside the vesicles. *Science* 302:618–622. doi:10.1126/science.1089904 ⚠ (paper confirmed ✔, DOI from memory) | [L] |

**Contested:** whether life began on clay, in hydrothermal vents, in small ponds, or elsewhere is still open. Use the clay story as a *plausible and productive* idea, not as fact.

## 3. What we can learn

**Keeping living material alive**
- **Living works fail in public.** *Victimless Leather* was switched off at MoMA after five weeks because it overgrew its incubator ✔. Plan maintenance, a named carer, and a dignified end (for example, firing) from the start [E].
- **Mould is the common enemy** in sealed organic set-ups. The Stardirt notes already record this for anthotypes in a camera obscura [E].
- **Choose robust organisms.** The successful ceramic projects used cyanobacteria (*Synechococcus*) and spore-forming *Bacillus* ✔ (Heveran 2020, Dutto 2024) [L].
- **Porosity is a design variable, not a constant.** In the terracotta and earthenware fuel cells, porosity and wall thickness changed both power and stability, and porous earthenware leaked liquid early on ✔ (Winfield 2013) [L]. Bisque temperature and clay body will matter.
- **Long durations are possible.** Fukasawa recorded for 100+ days and Dranseike cultured for over a year ✔ [L]. A 12-month residency fits that timescale.

**How the science was presented**
- **Overclaiming spreads faster than corrections.** The fungal "50 words" headline (2022) was widely reported. The critique (Blatt et al. 2024) got far less attention ✔.
- **Old myths come back.** The idea that pottery records sound from the moment it was made (Woodbridge, *Proc. IEEE*, 1969) was tested by later researchers and by MythBusters, with no intelligible speech recovered ✔ ([overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeoacoustics)). The proposal should state clearly what fired clay does and does not keep (section 8).
- **Ambitious visions can outrun data.** The *Living Architecture* wall was announced as "high-risk"; this search found no final report ⚠.

**What worked**
- The scientist as a named co-author (Miranda and Braund; Giraud with MNHN).
- The process itself as the artwork: Huyghe let living systems drive the installation instead of illustrating science.
- Open, documented failure (ceramic cyanotype practitioners sharing wash-out problems, as noted in Stardirt).

**Lesson for this project:** say "we measure humidity, light and voltage, and we *choose* to hear them as the vessel's activity" instead of "the vessel speaks". That honesty is a strength, and the call's audience will notice it.

## 5. Mission and vision

> **Clay remembers. Living things respond. We listen, and we say how.**
>
> Every clay is local. Its minerals, its colour and the life inside it come from one place and a very long history. Every clay is also universal. Its elements were made in stars, and it forms by the same slow weathering everywhere on Earth. Clay may have sheltered life's first molecules, it still shelters microbes in every soil, and people later used it to keep their first records.
>
> In Asturias we dig clay at several sites, with the people who know them, and let each clay's own living community grow in vessels made from it. Each vessel has a small microcontroller that listens to slow signals (moisture, light, gas, voltage) and voices them as light and sound. The vessels talk to their neighbours, not to a central brain. Every vessel follows the same rules, so what differs is the place.
>
> Over the year the communities leave traces in the clay. At the end some vessels are fired, and the living record becomes a mineral one. We do not decode a language or claim that the vessel thinks. We show, step by step, how relationships between mineral, microbe, machine and people become perceivable, and where our own choices shape what is heard.

# Part C: Research base

## 6. What has been done in engineering

- **Living ceramics.** Porous clay-based ceramics host cyanobacteria and engineered bacteria. Capillary action pumps nutrients through the pores ✔ (Dutto 2024) [L]. Low-temperature sol–gel "biocers" keep microbes viable inside the matrix ✔ (Soltmann 2004) [L]. *Not yet shown:* ordinary studio bisque or raw clay used for months outside the lab.
- **Living building materials.** Cyanobacteria precipitate calcium carbonate that binds sand. Viability was kept for 30 days, and the material regrew three times ✔ (Heveran 2020) [L]. Printed cyanobacteria were cultured for over a year ✔ (Dranseike 2025) [L].
- **Biofabrication and bioprinting.** Bacterial inks can be printed (Flink) ✔ (Schaffner 2017) [L]. Volumetric bioprinting at Utrecht prints cell-laden gels in seconds, mainly for medicine ✔ ([UU profile, Levato](https://www.uu.nl/staff/RLevato)) [L]. Your notes found no published work on printing living cells onto fired clay ⚠. This search did not find any either.
- **Microcontroller and electronic sensing of living systems.**
  - Microbial fuel cells with **fired clay** walls give a voltage that tracks microbial activity ✔ (Winfield 2013) [L]. This is the most direct way to "listen" electrically to a colony on clay.
  - Fungal spiking is recorded with needle electrodes and high-impedance amplifiers over 100+ days ✔ (Fukasawa 2024; Adamatzky) [L]. Signals are microvolts to millivolts and need careful shielding (see the Blatt critique).
  - Low-cost proxies that are well established in electronics [E]: humidity and temperature (for example, SHT3x sensors), CO₂ for photosynthesis or respiration (for example, NDIR SCD4x), spectral colour (Ward already uses a spectral sensor in the Ceram tile work), time-lapse imaging, and ADC voltage logging (for example, ADS1115). Specific part choices are suggestions ⚠.
  - Plant electrophysiology kits (for example, Backyard Brains' Plant SpikerBox) show that hobby-grade hardware can record biological voltages ⚠.

## 7. What has been done in biology

- **Clay helps microbes.** It buffers pH, supplies minerals, and stimulates respiration ✔ (Stotzky and Rem 1966) [E]. It reduces heavy-metal toxicity ⚠ (Babich and Stotzky 1977) [L]. It shields phage and nucleic acids from UV ⚠ [L]. *Limit:* most UV tests used 254 nm (germicidal UV), not sunlight. There is no direct test of cyanobacterial mats on clay under natural UV (your notes) [H].
- **Microbes change clay.** Microbial activity can help transform smectite into illite and precipitate carbonates ⚠ (Fiore, CNR; *Minerals* 2025 review; titles to confirm) [L to E].
- **Ceramics get colonised.** On exposed porous ceramics, phototrophs (cyanobacteria, algae) arrive first, then bacteria and fungi ⚠ (*Minerals* 2025 review) [E for biodeterioration]. This works in the project's favour: colonisation is likely. It also carries a risk: unwanted organisms will arrive too.
- **Raw earth already holds microbes.** In-situ earthen walls hold lower-diversity communities, shaped by building history and room use. No harmful species were singled out, but humid conditions can favour pathogens ✔ (Simons 2020) [E].
- **Basal cognition in microbes.** Biofilm electrical signalling ✔ (Prindle 2015) [L]. Quorum sensing, where bacteria coordinate by chemical signals ⚠ (Miller and Bassler, *Annu. Rev. Microbiol.* 55:165, 2001) [E]. Slime-mould problem solving ✔ (Nakagaki 2000; Tero 2010) [L]. Fungal "ecological memory", where a network's shape reflects past food ⚠ (Fukasawa, Savoury, Boddy, *ISME J* 2020) [L]. The cognitive *interpretation* is [H] and contested (Adams 2018).

**The point (Ward, 4 November 2026):** a cyanobacterial fossil record of two billion years is "something else than a clay tablet". People's clay records cover about 10,000 years. The planet's living records cover billions. The vessels sit between the two: a human-made clay object that holds a living record.

**An iron link to Stardirt:** the oxygen made by cyanobacteria "rusted" the Earth. Iron dissolved in the oceans was oxidised and settled out (banded iron formations), and red, oxidised iron became common in rocks and soils after the oxygen rise [E] ⚠. Much of the red in red clays is oxidised iron, so part of the colour of terracotta is, in a sense, a long legacy of cyanobacterial oxygen [H, framing]. Note that kiln firing in an oxidising atmosphere also turns iron red, so check this before using it.

**Takeaway:** fired clay is an excellent record of *physical and chemical* events (heat, magnetism, absorbed substances, pressed marks). It is not a record of sound or meaning. A living colony's trace is likely to survive firing only as mineral residue [H].

## 12. Local and universal: clay sites in Asturias

**Why "local" is real.**
- Each clay's minerals, iron content, colour and firing behaviour come from its parent rock and history [E].
- Its living community depends on place and use. In earthen walls, building history mattered more than clay composition ✔ (Simons 2020) [E].

**Why "universal" is real.**
- The elements in clay (silicon, oxygen, aluminium, iron) were made in stars [E].
- Clay minerals form by the same weathering of rock everywhere [E].
- A global survey of 237 soils on six continents found that **about 2% of bacterial types (roughly 500) make up almost half of soil bacterial communities worldwide** ✔ (Delgado-Baquerizo et al., *Science* 359:320–325, 2018, doi:10.1126/science.aap9516) [E, one large study].
- So soils are unique in their mix and history, and share a common core. That core is the bridge from local to universal.

### 12a. Deep dive: where do soil bacteria do best, and does it match clay?

**What the 2018 global atlas actually found** (Delgado-Baquerizo et al., *Science* 359:320–325, [doi:10.1126/science.aap9516](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aap9516)) ✔. The full text could not be opened in this session, so these details come from abstracts, figure captions and press coverage.
- 237 sites on six continents; 16S rRNA gene sequencing; phylotypes defined at 97% sequence similarity.
- **511 of 25,224 phylotypes (2%) were "dominant"**, meaning both abundant and found nearly everywhere. Together they made up almost half of the bacterial communities [E, one large study].
- The dominant phylotypes group into ecological clusters with shared habitat preferences: **high pH, low pH, drylands, low plant productivity**. A press summary mentions a fifth "dry forest" group, so the sources disagree on the number ⚠.
- The best predictors were **soil pH, aridity and climate, and plant productivity**. Soil texture and carbon were also tested ⚠ (variance explained not checked).
- **Important reading:** this study is about *which types* dominate everywhere, not about *where* bacteria are concentrated. It says the same few hundred types live almost everywhere, and which of them lead depends mostly on pH and dryness.

**A striking example of a dominant type: "DA101", *Candidatus* Udaeobacter copiosus** (Verrucomicrobia, Spartobacteria). It was found in more than 1,000 soils and is often the single most abundant type, especially in grasslands. It has a very small genome (about 2.8 Mb versus about 4.7 Mb typical) and **cannot make several amino acids and vitamins itself**, so it relies on its neighbours ✔ (Brewer et al., *Nature Microbiology* 2:16198, 2016) [L]. **For the Dirty Lab framing, this is a good point: the most successful soil bacterium is one that cannot live alone.** It also has not yet been grown as a pure culture in the lab ⚠ (check).

**Where bacteria do best (the question behind the atlas):**
- **pH is the strongest single predictor of bacterial diversity** across 98 soils in the Americas. Diversity peaks near neutral pH and is lowest in acid soils ✔ (Fierer and Jackson, *PNAS* 103:626–631, 2006, doi:10.1073/pnas.0507535103) [E, replicated].
- **Finer soil particles hold more bacterial diversity.** The silt (2–63 µm) and **clay (<2 µm)** fractions had higher diversity than sand; coarse fractions were poorer because of low nutrients, grazing and fungal competition ✔ (Sessitsch et al., *Appl. Environ. Microbiol.* 67:4215–4224, 2001, doi:10.1128/AEM.67.9.4215-4224.2001) [L to E].
- **More clay, more microbial biomass.** Across about 490 German soils, clay content correlated positively with microbial biomass carbon, and the authors suggest clay *protects* microbial biomass ⚠ (Müller and Höper, *Soil Biol. Biochem.* 36:877, 2004; found via search, details not checked) [E as a correlation]. The effect depends on the food source, so it is not universal.
- **At the micro scale, bacteria live in patches** and interact with only a few neighbours ✔ (Raynaud and Nunan, *PLoS ONE* 9:e87217, 2014) [L].
- **Where most bacteria are on Earth:** about 70 Gt of carbon in bacteria in total, most of it in the **deep subsurface**, not in topsoil ✔ (Bar-On, Phillips and Milo, *PNAS* 115:6506, 2018; split between soil and subsurface ⚠).

**So does "the best environment" match clay?** Partly, and with a twist.
- **Yes, in soil:** clay-sized particles and clay-rich soils host more diversity and more biomass, likely through surface area, water holding, nutrient binding and protection (Stotzky, Sessitsch, Müller and Höper) [E as correlation, mechanism H].
- **But heavy clay that stays waterlogged loses oxygen** and favours different (anaerobic) communities [E, general soil science ⚠].
- **And the twist for the vessels: firing changes clay.** Above roughly 500–600 °C clay minerals lose their structural water (kaolinite becomes metakaolin), so a bisque vessel is no longer a clay *mineral* in the soil sense. Much of the surface chemistry that helps microbes in soil (cation exchange, swelling layers) is reduced. Porosity remains [E, ceramics ⚠ cite a ceramics text].
- **Design consequence [H]:** compare three states of the same local clay, namely **raw** (unfired, sun-dried), **low bisque** and **higher bisque**, or line a bisque vessel with a layer of raw local clay. This tests whether the "soil-like" clay surface or just the porous structure matters, and it adds a strong idea: *firing is the moment the clay stops being a habitat and becomes a record.*

### 12b. Cyanobacteria: the oldest life you can see, and how they meet clay

*Suggested by Ward. Starting page: [UCMP Berkeley, Introduction to the Cyanobacteria](https://ucmp.berkeley.edu/bacteria/cyanointro.html) (blocked in this session, so not read).*
*General reference kept by Ward: [Wikipedia, "Cyanobacteria"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyanobacteria). Use it as an overview and to find primary sources, not as a citation in the proposal.*

**Names:** "blue-green algae" is the old common name for cyanobacteria. It is the same group, but the name is misleading: they are **bacteria**, not algae. True algae are eukaryotes (cells with a nucleus) [E].

**What you see on water and land** [E, general; ⚠ check a field guide]:
- **On lakes and ponds in summer:** a cyanobacterial bloom looks like spilled blue-green paint, pea soup or a green film, often with a musty smell (common genera: *Microcystis*, *Dolichospermum*/*Anabaena*, *Aphanizomenon*). But not all green water is cyanobacteria. Stringy, hair-like green mats are usually true green algae, and small floating leaves are duckweed (a plant).
- **Field safety:** many blooms make **cyanotoxins**, which can harm people and kill dogs. Do not touch or sample a bloom without gloves and advice from the biology partner [E].
- **On land:** dark green to black crusts on soil, black streaks on walls and roof tiles, green films on flower pots, and *Nostoc*, a jelly-like colony that swells up after rain.

**Why they make a good opening**
- **Visible to the naked eye as communities.** A single cell is microscopic, but cyanobacteria form green and blue-green films, mats, jelly colonies (*Nostoc*) and blooms, and they built **stromatolites**, layered rocks that still grow today (for example at Shark Bay, Australia) [E] ⚠.
- **Among the oldest traces of life.** Layered stromatolite-like structures in the Strelley Pool Chert, Australia, are about **3.43 billion years** old ✔ (Allwood et al., *Nature* 441:714–718, 2006, doi:10.1038/nature04764) [L]. **Contested:** their biological origin is debated, and very old stromatolites need not have been made by cyanobacteria (other microbes also build mats).
- **They changed the planet.** Cyanobacteria invented oxygen-producing photosynthesis [E]. Oxygen rose in the atmosphere during the **Great Oxidation Event, roughly 2.4 to 2.3 billion years ago** [E]. **Contested:** whether cyanobacteria appeared shortly before that or hundreds of millions of years earlier. Estimates for their origin range from about 2.7 to 3.4 billion years ago ⚠ (biomarker and molecular-clock studies, debated).
- **A correction to the intro wording:** life already existed long before the oxygen rise. Oxygen did not let life *start*. It made aerobic metabolism, the ozone shield and, much later, complex life possible [E]. It was also toxic to many existing microbes [E].
- **Every plant carries one.** Chloroplasts descend from a cyanobacterium taken in by an early cell (endosymbiosis) [E] ⚠ (Margulis 1967 and later molecular work; cite a review).
- **They keep time.** Cyanobacteria have a 24-hour circadian clock, and its core (three proteins, KaiA, KaiB and KaiC) keeps ticking even in a test tube ⚠ (Nakajima et al., *Science* 308:414, 2005; check). This is a clear, non-overclaimed example of "basal intelligence": anticipating dawn.

**How cyanobacteria and clay are linked** (from strongest to weakest evidence)
| Link | What is known | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **They bind soil and clay** | In desert soil crusts, the cyanobacterium *Microcoleus vaginatus* winds sticky sheaths through the soil and holds particles together, with clay particles attached to the sheaths. This improves stability, moisture and fertility. | [E] | ✔ Belnap and Gardner, *Great Basin Naturalist* 53:40–47 (1993) ([BYU](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/gbn/vol53/iss1/6)) |
| **They make clay** | In salt lakes (Salar de Llamara, Chile; Great Salt Lake), magnesium clays (kerolite, stevensite) form on the slime (EPS) and sheaths of cyanobacterial mats. The clay can then mineralise the microbes. | [L] | ✔ (summaries; e.g. *Communications Earth & Environment* 2022, doi:10.1038/s43247-022-00658-5) |
| **Clay preserves them as fossils** | In lab experiments, filamentous cyanobacteria were coated in **clay mineral veneers** within a week, which helps them fossilise. | [L] | ✔ Newman et al., *Geobiology* 15:484–498 (2017), doi:10.1111/gbi.12229 |
| **Clay is used to remove their blooms** | Modified clays are sprayed on water to sink harmful blooms. Removal is very effective, but sediment loads and freshwater results are debated. | [E] (practice) | ✔ Yu et al., *Harmful Algae* review (2017) ([PDF](https://www2.whoi.edu/site/andersonlab/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2018/10/Yu-et-al-Review-Mitigation-of-harmful-algal-blooms-using-modified-clays_2017.pdf)) |
| **Land life, more clay, more oxygen** | Kennedy et al. proposed that early land microbes boosted clay formation in soils about 800 to 600 million years ago, and that clay buried organic carbon, helping oxygen rise before animals appeared. | [H], **debated** | ✔ Kennedy et al., *Science* 311:1446–1449 (2006), doi:10.1126/science.1118929 |
| **Clay shielded them from UV** | Clay protects molecules and phage from UV in the lab. No direct test on cyanobacteria under sunlight. | [H] | Section 7 |
| **They are the first colonisers of ceramics** | On exposed porous ceramics, phototrophs such as cyanobacteria arrive first. | [E] for biodeterioration | ⚠ *Minerals* 2025 review |

**The story in one line [H, framing]:** *Cyanobacteria and clay have shaped each other for billions of years. The microbes bind, build and are preserved by clay, and clay holds the carbon that let oxygen rise.*

**What this means for the vessels [H, design]**
- Cyanobacteria are likely to appear in most site samples anyway, because they are the first colonisers of porous ceramic.
- Their activity is **visible** (green colour) and **rhythmic** (day and night), so it suits simple sensors: a colour or spectral sensor for chlorophyll, light, and CO₂ or O₂ that rises and falls with photosynthesis. Their day and night rhythm can become the "beat" of the score.
- This fits the simplified message: *the oldest planet-changers, singing in clay from different places.*

### 12c. Cyanobacteria: what people use them for, where they live near you, and links to clay and ceramics

**Local presence (Belgium) [E, general; ⚠ details not checked]**
- *Blauwalg* (cyanobacterial blooms) appears in Flemish lakes and ponds in summer, Bruges included. The Flemish environment agency (VMM) monitors it, and swimming waters close when blooms are toxic.
- Blooms need warm, still, nutrient-rich water, and warmer summers make them more frequent ⚠ (Paerl and Huisman, "Blooms like it hot", *Science* 2008).
- In winter there are no blooms, but cyanobacteria remain in soil, on stones, walls and pots, and as resting cells in lake sediment ⚠. **For the winter pilot, sample soil and pot surfaces, not bloom water. It is also safer.**

**What people use cyanobacteria for (in general) [E unless noted; ⚠ sources to add]**
- **Food:** *Spirulina* (*Arthrospira*) powder and tablets. Traditionally harvested at Lake Chad and by the Aztecs at Lake Texcoco. *Nostoc* is also eaten in parts of Asia.
- **Blue colour:** phycocyanin from spirulina is used as a natural blue food colouring.
- **Fertiliser:** nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria (*Anabaena*, living inside the water fern *Azolla*) have long been used in rice paddies.
- **Soil repair:** inoculating dry land with crust-forming cyanobacteria to stop erosion (see 12b; [L] to field trials).
- **Research and biotech:** *Synechocystis* and *Synechococcus* are model organisms for photosynthesis, the circadian clock, and fuels or chemicals made from light and CO₂ [L].

**Links to clay and ceramics** (new this round; mostly lab-scale)
